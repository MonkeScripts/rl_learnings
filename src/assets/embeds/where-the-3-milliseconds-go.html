Where the 3 milliseconds go

Two instruments measure the station-keeping policy's inference cost, and they disagree by a factor of three. The gap between them is the finding.

The two instruments

Neither one measures “the inference cost” on its own. They measure different things, and knowing which is which is the whole trick.

Instrument 1 · the flight recorder

The us column

The policy task takes a timestamp when it wakes and another after the forward pass, and the difference rides out on every recorded row. One value per inference, 8 769 of them in a 90 second run.

Sees: real conditions. Cold caches, the control tick landing mid-inference, whatever else the robot was doing.

Cannot see: the split. It is one wall-clock number, and a 500 Hz tick that interrupts the inference is counted inside it.

Instrument 2 · the console profiler

Console key B

Disarmed, it runs the forward pass, then 128 bare tanh calls, then the 40 divides that open the pass, 200 times each. It reports the fastest run of each and subtracts to get the matrix work.

Sees: the parts. Which line of the network costs what.

Cannot see: real conditions. Running 200 passes back to back leaves the code resident in cache, which is the one thing a real inference never gets.

Why the fastest run, and not the average

Every timing sample on this chip is the true cost plus whatever interrupted it. Interruptions only ever add, so the distribution has a hard floor and a long tail. An average is an average of interruptions and moves with system load; the minimum over enough samples is the closest thing to the cost itself.

That logic runs in both instruments and is why they can be compared. The recorder's minimum of 1 364 µs is a real inference with as little interference as 8 769 tries could find. The profiler's 435 µs is the same arithmetic with the code already in cache. Both are floors, so the 929 µs between them is real and needs a cause.

Why the gap points at the cache

Every piece of the inference path lives in flash and is reached through a 32 KB instruction cache: the forward pass at 0x400e4a68, tanhf at 0x4018c148, expm1f just past it, and the software float divide out at 0x401a8d58. Those span roughly a megabyte.

The profiler runs 200 passes back to back, so it pays for that once. A real inference runs once every 10 milliseconds, and in the gap the control tick, the motor commutation and the radio all execute their own flash code. This robot has been bitten by exactly this before: in September the weights themselves sat in flash and thrashed the cache until they were forced into RAM. That fix moved the data. The code was never moved.

What each lever is actually worth

Against a real inference of 1 364 µs, not against the 435 µs the profiler sees.

Move the forward pass into RAM

up to 929 µs

Changes no arithmetic whatsoever, costs about 340 bytes, and is the experiment that confirms or kills the cache attribution. Reflash, recapture, and compare the us minimum against 1 364.

Approximate tanh in the code generator

255 µs

The largest single piece of the arithmetic, and it also removes three calls out into distant flash, so it helps the cache term too. This one changes the numbers the network produces, so the golden replay tolerance of 2e-4 has to be decided first rather than discovered afterwards.

Quantize to int16 with esp-dl

169 µs

Twelve percent of a real inference. In exchange: quantization error in a control loop, a flatbuffers loader, a graph interpreter, a second binary in the partition table, and the loss of a bit-exact replay gate. The two larger terms would still be there afterwards.

What would change these numbers

The 929 µs is an attribution, not a reading. It is what remains after subtracting one instrument from another. The cache is the best explanation available, and the RAM experiment is what would settle it.

The profiler had a fixture bug on its first run. It fed the observation assembler an all-zero input, and tanhf returns early for tiny arguments without ever reaching expm1f. That timed the cheap branch and made a step read faster than the forward pass inside it. It now runs on the inputs from the median-cost row of the capture.

Faster is not automatically better here. The policy trained with an action delay drawn from 5 to 30 ms and the robot already flies at 6 to 9. Removing 2 ms moves it below anything it saw in training, at the fast edge rather than toward the middle.
